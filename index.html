Catapult DOE Lab

Choose 2 to 8 factors with 1 to 3 levels each, run a factorial (full or fractional) and a Taguchi orthogonal array on the same catapult, then confirm which setting you can trust.

Why run a designed experiment?

Changing one factor at a time (OFAT) is slow and blind to interactions. A designed experiment changes several factors together in a planned pattern, so every shot tells you about every factor.

Factorial: full or fractional

A full factorial runs every combination of every level: 4 factors at 2 levels need 16 runs, but 8 factors at 3 levels would need 6,561. So the lab switches to a fractional factorial as the run count grows: a carefully chosen fraction (81 runs for eight 3-level factors) that keeps the main effects, and as many interactions as it can, separable.

The price of a fraction is aliasing: some effects share a column and cannot be told apart. The resolution (III, IV, V) says which. Small designs stay full, and you can force a full factorial up to 256 runs.

Taguchi orthogonal array

A small, balanced subset of the full factorial. The lab picks the smallest standard array that fits your factors: L4, L8, L9, L12, L16, L18 or L27.

Each run gets a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio that rewards a setting that is on target and repeatable. Spare columns in the array act as the error estimate. Arrays here estimate main effects only; use the factorial to see interactions. If a mixed 2- and 3-level design would put a two-level factor in a three-level column, the Taguchi tab warns that it is unbalanced and offers an exactly balanced (larger) array.

Confirmation run

Every model is a prediction. Fire 10 more shots at the recommended setting. The lab reports three separate checks: how close the observed mean is to the predicted mean, an approximate statistical test of that gap, and whether enough shots landed in the tolerance band. Adopt the setting only if the practical check passes.

The eight catapult factors

Pick how many to use (2 to 8) on the Factorial or Taguchi tab. In the experiments, factors you do not use stay fixed at their baseline; the Play tab always shows all eight.

Because the physics multiplies these factors (distance ∝ θ² · stiffness · sin 2α / mass), the factors interact: the benefit of a bigger pull-back depends on the ball mass, the hook and the bands. That is exactly what an OFAT trial cannot see.

Terms and formulas

Factor, level
A setting you control, and the values you test it at. A factor with 1 level is held constant; with 2 or 3 levels it is varied.
Run, replicate
One combination of levels; replicates are repeat shots at the same combination. Replicates measure the noise.
Main effect
How the average response changes as a factor moves through its levels.
Interaction
The effect of one factor changes depending on the level of another. On a plot, the lines are not parallel.
% contribution
Share of the total variation explained by a term (SS term ÷ SS total).
S/N ratio
Taguchi's score per run. Higher is always better.
Adjustment factor
A factor that moves the mean but barely changes the S/N. Use it to land on target.

Suggested 90-minute session

  1. Play (10 min). Tune the catapult by feel to hit the target. Count shots used.
  2. Factorial (30 min). Run every combination. Read Pareto, main effects, then interactions.
  3. Taguchi (25 min). Run the array. Read the S/N response table and factor roles.
  4. Confirm (10 min). Ten shots at each recommended setting.
  5. Decide (15 min). Compare cost, accuracy and repeatability. Reveal the hidden physics last.

Using a real catapult? Every response cell accepts typed values, so students can enter their own measured distances instead of simulated ones.
